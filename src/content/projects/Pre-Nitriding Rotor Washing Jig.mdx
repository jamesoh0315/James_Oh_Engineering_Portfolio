During my co-op term at **Dometic Marine**, actuator rotors had to be scrubbed
completely free of grease and machining debris before nitriding, and operators did this
by holding each rotor against a rotating brush with their hands in the water. Building on
an early-stage concept from a previous term, I designed the **quick-locking fixture** that
went into production.

## Overview

### The Problem

Nitriding diffuses nitrogen into the surface of a part to harden it. Any residual grease or
machining debris left on a rotor **blocks that absorption**, so the rotors have to be
spotless before they reach the furnace.

Cleaning happens in an **ultrasonic bath**, with a pneumatic rotating brush run down the
rotor's internal threads. The original process had operators hold each rotor against that
brush themselves, submerged, which carried a **repetitive stress injury risk** and
cleaned the internal threads inconsistently.

There are two rotor families that run through the same station, **AMS (45 mm)** and
**Gyro**, so the jig exists in two variants.

### Design Objectives

- **Hands out of the water** — the operator must be able to lock and release a rotor
  without reaching into the bath.
- **Survive the bath** — the fixture lives in a heated ultrasonic tank, so it has to hold
  up thermally, chemically, and mechanically in that environment.
- **Isolate the wear** — something is always going to be scraped by the rotors. Better that
  it is one cheap part chosen in advance than the whole assembly.

## Design overview
### The Jig

Each variant is a pair of **towers** on a base plate that drops into the tank. The rotor
sits down into a tower, and the operator closes the top arm so a small **nub** seats into a
slot machined to stop the rotor from spinning against the rotating brush. A **locking bar**
pushes down to secure the arm, and the whole sequence reverses to unload.

### Material selection

The tank is heated, chemically active, and full of parts being moved around. I tested
**ABS**, **ASA**, and **PET-CF** for thermal, chemical, and mechanical resistance in the
heated bath, and selected **ASA** as the best balance of durability against cost.

## Field failures and fixes

The jig held up ergonomically, and operators liked it. However, occasional failures led
to some redesign.

### Locking arm fracture

The handle's threaded rod **ripped through the printed arm** due to excessive force during use.

The root cause was simply that the thin arm piece let the long threaded rod leverage the
force and rip through the part, so the arm was **thickened to 1″** and the mating slot on
the holder opened up to match. That thickness wasn't arbitrary: **1″ is a common stock
size**, so when this part eventually moves to machined aluminum, the machinist never has
to face that surface at all.

### Base holder fracture

A base holder shattered around the rotor bore. The most likely cause was an operator
untrained on the station forcing the arm outward against its travel.

The fix was **1/8″ fillets** on the internal corners to break up the stress concentration,
plus thicker walls through that region, applied to both the top and bottom holders, since
they share the same geometry and the same weakness.

### The wear part

The nubs as designed were wearing out, requiring replacements every two weeks. They
scrape the rotor slot on every cycle, so concentrating the wear there means the
**cheapest and fastest-replaced part** in the assembly is the one that degrades.

## Outcome

Both variants are in **daily production use**, deployed for **under $350** in total,
including the handle, threaded rods, base, filament, fasteners, etc.

To keep the process consistent between operators, I authored a **work standard** covering
the full load, wash, and unload sequence, which brought down the rate of parts needing a
repeat cleaning.

### Next steps

- A **machined aluminum version**, which the current design is already shaped for: 1″ arm
  stock, internal fillets at common endmill radii, helicoiled threads, and a 4.25″ round
  stock start for the Gyro top piece.
- A lift handle so the whole assembly can come out of the tank without anyone reaching into
  the water.

### Takeaways

- **Start supplier conversations earlier.** RFQs, POs, and back-and-forth with machine
  shops can take up to several weeks. I realized too late in the term to get the aluminum
  version quoted, and that is the thing I would do differently.
- **Decide in advance which part is allowed to fail.** Despite not having a permanent
  aluminum version of the jig, isolating the wear to the nub has greatly increased the
  longevity of 3D printed components.
