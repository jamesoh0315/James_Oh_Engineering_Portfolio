During my 4 month intership term at **Dometic Marine**, I faced a problem which a CNC lathe's polyurethane spindle liner absorbs coolant over time, swells, and
**seizes in the spindle**. Freeing one takes dry ice, partial disassembly of the machine,
and over half an hour of downtime. I designed a **hybrid liner** that splits the
assembly in two, so the removable section pulls out through the chuck in **under five
minutes**, no disassembly, no dry ice.

## Overview
### The Problem

**Spindle liners** reduce the inner diameter of a CNC lathe spindle to closely match the
bar stock being machined, suppressing vibration and allowing for tighter tolerances.
Polyurethane liners are preferred over metal ones for their **superior vibration
damping** and the protection they give to bar stock surfaces. Over time, however,
they **absorb coolant, swell, and seize** inside the spindle.

Freeing a seized liner takes **dry ice** and **partial disassembly** of the machine — a
procedure that costs **hours of downtime** on every changeover.

### The Concept

The hybrid spindle liner splits the assembly in two: a **metal liner that stays
permanently** in the spindle, and a **short removable polyurethane section** at the
front. Placing the polyurethane nearer the chuck means the worn section can be drawn out
through the chuck itself: changeovers happen from the front of the machine,
**without disassembly or dry ice**.

### Design Constraints

- All mechanisms must **fit within the lathe's inner drawtube diameter**.
- The polyurethane liner section must be accessible from the chuck side **without machine disassembly.**
- Installation and removal must work through a quick-change mechanism **without bolts**, as access to the inner drawtube is very limited.
- Bar stock being machined must **only contact the polyurethane** liner.


## Approach
### Initial Concept Generation

Before opening CAD, I worked through concepts on paper. The **coupling interface**
between the polyurethane and metal sections was the decision everything else
depended on, so I surveyed existing mechanisms suited to the application and weighed
each against **operational viability, reliability, and the constraints above**.

Three concepts came out of that process.

### 3D Print Prototypes

To build a proof of concept, I prototyped and iterated the bayonet coupling using 3D
printing. Across more than three versions I varied the **J-slot geometry**, the spring,
and the locking pin position, working toward a positive **twist-to-lock feel**.

I designed for **DFM and serviceability** alongside function: I shaped one component to keep
chips out of the inner spring, and the J-slot stays **machinable on a 4-axis CNC**.

### Assembly Planning

With the coupling mechanism settled, I turned to fitting the assembly into the
machine. That meant measuring the **inner drawtube diameter, its length, and the
depth of the chuck**, then cross-checking them against the
**manufacturer's specifications**. Those numbers set the **total assembly length**
and the individual dimensions of the polyurethane and metal sections.

### Installation / Removal Tool

To assist with installation and removal, I designed and fabricated a custom
tool. It pairs a **3D-printed handle and end fitting** with a length of **production
scrap rod** that I post-processed in the machine shop, incurring **no extra material cost.**

## Outcome

Before the end of my internship term, I delivered a functional prototype projected to
cut spindle liner changeovers **from over 30 minutes to under 5**. Making the
polyurethane section modular also opened a second opportunity: with that section now
customizable, its internal bore can be profiled into a nozzle cone to improve
**coolant flow**.

filed for a patent** at the end-of-term presentation.

of the coupling, alongside internal documentation covering the design rationale and
the next steps forward.
