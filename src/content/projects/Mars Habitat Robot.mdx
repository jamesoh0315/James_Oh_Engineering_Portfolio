**ENPH 253** is a 6 week summer course where teams build an autonomous robot from nothing
and then race it head to head. The 2026 brief was a simulated Mars construction site: cross
rough terrain, assemble a habitat, uncover a solar panel, recover a metal-bearing rock, and
find the Teletubbies hiding behind the boulders. Then the robots compete autonomously in two minute heats. 

## Overview

### The competition

The course is an 8 by 8 ft board with a black tape path, a ramp, and a floor raised 6″ above
the lower level. Robots score by assembling a four-piece habitat dome, stacking nesting tower
tubes, pulling a fabric cover off a solar panel, recovering the one boulder with aluminium
hidden inside it, and pointing out two Teletubbies.

The Teletubbies carry a rule worth noting, because it drove the entire vision design: a robot
announces one by flashing a light at least three times, and **a single false alarm nulls every
Teletubby point you have earned**.

### Strategy and my scope

Our team's proposal set a deliberate target: **reliably score six points** rather than chase all
five objectives and finish with nothing working.

This was a three-person project. My scope was the **mechanical design and fabrication**, the
**vision system**, the **custom PCBs**, and the **electromechanical modules**.

### Chassis and drivetrain

Both the wheelbase and the track came out at **10″**, each for its own reason. Short enough
that the robot does not bottom out crossing the ramp break. Long enough to resist tipping
under hard acceleration. Narrow enough that the overall width stays under 12" to clear the narrowest
section of the course. 

Ground clearance follows from the ramp geometry. Bridging a break angle
needs at least **0.3112″** underneath, which set the floor for wheel size.

Sizing the drive was a torque problem. At an estimated **3 kg** and 85% drivetrain efficiency,
each wheel needs about **0.089 Nm** to climb the 7.1° ramp, against **0.24 Nm** of stall torque
per motor. That is a **2.7× margin**, which then sets the speed: roughly 281 rpm under load,
about **1.21 m/s**, enough to cover the 18 ft tape run in **7.5 s** while cruising at 60% of
maximum.

We had a line-following sensor suite on board, but the surface wasn't always taped, so the robot still needed to navigate on its own. We combined the two motors' built-in encoders with an **Inertial Measurement Unit (IMU)** for odometry, which held our position error under 0.5 inches.

For packaging, we used timing belts to put distance between the motors and the driven axle, freeing up room for the grabber mechanism at the rear. The detail I'm most happy with is the **slotted motor brackets**: the slots let the motor shift position during assembly, so belts went on and tensioned easily instead of fighting a fixed mounting point.

The habitat grabber was shaped to match the dome's internal profile, lifting pieces into place on a **linear rack and pinion**. Since navigation wasn't always perfect, we added **snap action switches** to sense the piece as it arrived and tell the robot exactly when to engage the lift.

By the time this mechanism came up, we'd already used up every pin on our power distribution board, so a second servo wasn't an option. That constraint led to a simple **4-bar linkage** arm, driven off a single servo, that swings out to the solar panel cover and recovers it with a passive claw that hooks on like a carabiner. It kept the electrical architecture simple and still met the packaging limits.

### Custom PCBs

Despite being the mechanical lead, my contribution to the robot's electrical system involved
designing custom H-bridge and voltage booster PCBs on KiCAD. 

The course **bans off-the-shelf H-bridge driver chips**, so the motor driver had to be built
from discrete parts. Unfortunately with only **NPN MOSFETs** available in the lab, switching the high side needs a gate voltage above the
motor rail. 

So the H-bridge board takes **two** inputs, 15V for the motors and 20V for gate drive, and a
voltage booster board exists purely to make that 20V. The booster is a **555-based boost converter**
feeding the bridge's 2N3904 drivers, which in turn drive the IRFZ44 power MOSFETs. Both boards
were laid out in KiCad and sent out with fab outputs and a BOM.

### Sensing and power

Tape following runs on **five IR phototransistors** straight into the ADC, with a BNO055 IMU
for heading and wheel encoders for distance. Switches go through Schmitt triggers so a noisy
edge cannot register twice.

Power is split deliberately. A **4S LiPo pack runs the motors, a 2S pack runs everything else**, and
the grounds meet at the battery negatives rather than anywhere convenient, so motor current
does not return through the sensing rails. Each load gets its own regulator, which is why an
ESP32-CAM current spike cannot brown out the main controller.

## Target detection

To minimize the computing power needed on the ESP32 board, the colorful target figures are found with **blob detection** with a fair amount of care going into filtering out noise.

Each frame gets converted to HSV, classified pixel by pixel, merged into fragments that belong to the same object, and then scored. A candidate is only accepted if it clears thresholds on area, fill percentage, and aspect ratio, and every rejection carries a named reason: `too-small`, `too-big`, `low-fill`, `bad-aspect`, `edge-of-range`.

That decision runs on **confidence score, not blob size**. A wall, a person, or a stretch of floor in roughly the right hue will out-area a small figure every time, so the confidence score instead combines HSV similarity, fill percentage, and aspect ratio into one number.

Lighting outside the lab is never as consistent as it is on a bench, so camera exposure calibration is folded into the robot's normal calibration routine rather than tuned once and left alone.
