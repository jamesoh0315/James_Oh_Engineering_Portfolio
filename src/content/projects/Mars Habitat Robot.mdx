**ENPH 253** is a six-week summer course where teams build an autonomous robot from nothing
and then race it head to head. The 2026 brief was a simulated Mars construction site: cross
rough terrain, assemble a habitat, uncover a solar panel, recover a metal-bearing rock, and
find the Teletubbies hiding behind the boulders. Two-minute heats, no remote control, a $200
budget, and three people.

## Overview

### The competition

The course is an 8 by 8 ft board with a black tape path, a ramp, and a floor raised 6″ above
the lower level. Robots score by assembling a four-piece habitat dome, stacking nesting tower
tubes, pulling a fabric cover off a solar panel, recovering the one boulder with aluminium
hidden inside it, and pointing out two Teletubbies.

The Teletubbies carry a rule worth noting, because it drove the entire vision design: a robot
announces one by flashing a light at least three times, and **a single false alarm nulls every
Teletubby point you have earned**.

### Strategy and my scope

The proposal set a deliberate target: **reliably score six points** rather than chase all
five objectives and finish with nothing working. That shaped what we built and what we left
alone. We never attempted the tower or the metal rock.

This was a three-person project. My scope was the **mechanical design and fabrication**, the
**vision system**, the **custom PCBs**, and the **electromechanical modules**. The navigation
and mission firmware that drives the robot around the course was a teammate's work, and the
robot only scores because that half works too.

### Chassis and drivetrain

Both the wheelbase and the track came out at **10″**, each for its own reason. Short enough
that the robot does not bottom out crossing the ramp break. Long enough to resist tipping
under hard acceleration. Narrow enough that the overall width stays under 12″, which matters
because the rules only promise 6″ of clearance between the tape and the boulders.

Ground clearance follows from the ramp geometry rather than taste. Bridging a break angle
needs at least **0.3112″** underneath, which set the floor for wheel size.

<Figures
	height={320}
	caption="Wheelbase and track, both 10″, each dimension driven by a different constraint."
	items={[

Sizing the drive was a torque problem. At an estimated **3 kg** and 85% drivetrain efficiency,
each wheel needs about **0.089 Nm** to climb the 7.1° ramp, against **0.24 Nm** of stall torque
per motor. That is a **2.7× margin**, which then sets the speed: roughly 281 rpm under load,
about **1.21 m/s**, enough to cover the 18 ft tape run in **7.5 s** while cruising at 60% of
maximum.

The detail I am most glad about is the **slotted motor brackets**. Rather than commit to a
gear ratio in CAD and hope the mesh was right, the slots let the motor position move after
assembly, and I printed a sweep of spacers from **7.5 mm to 17 mm in half-millimetre steps** to
set the centre distance empirically on the built robot. Gear mesh is exactly the kind of thing
that is easier to dial in by hand than to predict.

### Arm and grabber

The habitat pieces sit on a ring and hold to each other magnetically, so placing one is not a
simple pick and drop. The arm has to bring a piece in, seat it, and rotate the pieces already
on the ring out of the way to make room for the next.

Lifting a **165 g** piece at the worst-case lever arm needs about **0.134 Nm**, which set the
servo choice.

**The built arm is not the proposed one.** The proposal specified a scooping grabber on a rack
and pinion. What went to competition is a belt-driven parallel linkage running on GT2 pulleys,
with a two-finger fork gripper on the end. The linkage keeps the gripper level through its
travel, which a single pivoting arm does not, and that matters when the thing you are carrying
has to stay square to a magnetic joint.

## Electronics

### Custom PCBs

The course **bans off-the-shelf H-bridge driver chips**, so the motor driver had to be built
from discrete parts. That constraint has a consequence that is easy to miss until you draw it:
with only **NPN** devices available, switching the high side needs a gate voltage above the
motor rail. You cannot turn the top of the bridge fully on from the same supply it is
switching.

So the H-bridge board takes **two** inputs, 15V for the motors and 20V for gate drive, and a
second board exists purely to make that 20V. The booster is a **555-based boost converter**
feeding the bridge's 2N3904 drivers, which in turn drive the IRFZ44 power MOSFETs. Both boards
were laid out in KiCad and sent out with fab outputs and a BOM.

### Sensing and power

Tape following runs on **five IR phototransistors** straight into the ADC, with a BNO055 IMU
for heading and wheel encoders for distance. Switches go through Schmitt triggers so a noisy
edge cannot register twice. Adding it up, the ESP32-S3 ends up at **21 of 28 GPIO**, 5 of 10
ADC1 channels, 2 of 4 pulse counters and 2 of 6 MCPWM units, which is worth knowing before you
find out the hard way that you have run out of timers.

Power is split deliberately. A **4S pack runs the motors, a 2S pack runs everything else**, and
the grounds meet at the battery negatives rather than anywhere convenient, so motor current
does not return through the sensing rails. Each load gets its own regulator, which is why an
ESP32-CAM current spike cannot brown out the main controller.

### Electromechanical modules

Two modules exist purely because a competition robot gets handled by a person under time
pressure. The **user input board** puts calibration and program selection on physical toggle
switches with an engraved panel, plus an LED and a speaker so the robot can announce a
Teletubby without anyone watching a laptop.

The **camera module** is an ESP32-CAM and its decoupling capacitors on protoboard, inside a
printed enclosure that keeps the TX/RX lines and the flashing header reachable. Reflashing the
camera never requires taking the robot apart, which matters when you are tuning vision at the
venue.

## Target detection

Everything about the vision system follows from one rule: a false alarm nulls every Teletubby
point. That makes this a **precision problem, not a recall problem**. A detector that finds one
of the two Teletubbies and is never wrong beats a detector that finds both and occasionally
shouts at a boulder.

The ESP32-CAM converts each frame to HSV, classifies pixels through a per-colour lookup table,
merges neighbouring fragments that belong to the same object, then scores what is left. A
candidate is only accepted if it passes on area, fill percentage and aspect ratio, and every
rejection carries a named reason: `too-small`, `too-big`, `low-fill`, `bad-aspect`,
`edge-of-range`.

The decision I would defend hardest is that **the highest-confidence blob wins, not the largest
one**. A wall, a person, or a stretch of floor in roughly the right hue will out-area a small
doll every time, so picking by size hands the frame to the background. Confidence combines how
closely the hue matches, how solidly the blob fills its own bounding box, and whether its
proportions look like a doll rather than a smear.

Those thresholds could never be set in a lab. The venue's lighting was not going to match the
bench, so the camera hosts a **Wi-Fi debug and tuning page** that shows the live frame, the
per-colour numbers, and which candidates were thrown out and why. Limits get set against the
actual course, then persist to flash. The banner across the top is a reminder to turn the radio
off before a heat.

## Outcome

The robot scored **six points across the competition rounds**, against a historical mean of
zero: **two for uncovering the solar panel, two for habitat pieces placed, and two for finding
both Teletubbies.**

### What I'd take away

- **Build the tuning interface before you need it.** The vision thresholds that worked on a
  bench were never going to survive the venue's lighting, and the thing that made them fixable
  was a debug page that showed why a candidate was rejected, not just whether it was. Being
  able to change the answer on site was worth more than getting it right in advance.
- **Leave a dimension adjustable when you know you are guessing.** Slotting the motor brackets
  and printing a sweep of spacers cost an afternoon and removed gear mesh from the list of
  things that could go wrong permanently. The alternative was reprinting a bracket every time
  the ratio changed.
- **Scope is a design decision.** Targeting six reliable points instead of all five objectives
  is why there was time to make the parts we did build work. Most teams scored zero.
